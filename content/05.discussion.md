## Aestivation, local refugia, short- or long-distance migration

In order to better understand how _Anopheles_ mosquitoes deal with the dry season in the Sahel, it is important to know what would be the impact on population structure within and between cohorts of each considered behaviour.
It is worth remembering that the behaviours that we consider are not individual behaviours per se but more population trends that affect several consecutive generations in the same year and generations through the years.
It is also important at this point to state that not all members of the cohort are expected to have the exact same behaviour and that the behaviour of a cohort is heavily affected by its local environment meaning that two cohorts from the Sahel might have different behaviours.

Local refugia [@Lehmann14] is the least disruptive behaviour.
Under local refugia, part of the cohort acts and survives during the dry season in a very similar way to how it would act and survive during the rainy season at a location that can be reached within the lifetime of a mosquitoe.
It is assumed that this location cannot accomodate the large populations observed during the rainy season but provides an environment suitable for the survival of much smaller populations. 
This, in particular, means that many generationsof mosquitoes , or even most given that the dry season is much longer than the rainy season, will live the entirety of their existence during the dry season. 

Aestivation [@Lehmann10] also entails the survival of a smaller population at the location, or near the location, where it can be found during the rainy season. We consider that a cohort aestivates if some of its members, instead of continuing life during the dry season in a normal way, become inactive for a long period allowing them to survive during all or almost all of the dry season.
In substance, an aestivating cohort reduces significantly the number of generations between two consecutive rainy seasons.

Short-distance migration [@Charlwood00] is a much more disruptive assumption. 
It is assumed that the entire population is eradicated when conditions become too harsh at the beginning of the dry season but that other populations, in more hsopitable locations that are not too distant, manage to survive.
At the beginning of the rainy season, when conditions become more hospitable even in the Sahel, part of these populations move to the now empty niche and settle there.

Long-distance migration [@Huestis19]  sees the cohort travel with the winds that also drive the change of weather.
A migrating cohort following this pattern would move great distances (over 500 km in each direction) and (potentially) interact with many more stationary cohorts along the way. 
Such a population could travel these long distances in a few hops, getting to temporarily more hospitable locations in advance for the next generation to be able to breed safely.
Alternatively, such a migrating cohort could always be around the frontier of the rain, moving south as the dry season establishes itself from north to south and the other way when the rainy season starts to expand northerly.

These various possible behaviours, or classes of behaviours, may have different effects on various metrics that can be used to compare them.
As an example, under short-distance migration, because the local population during the wet season is completely eradicated, the population at the beginning of the next rainy season would look identical to a population (or a mix of populations, possibly) from a nearby location that was more hospitable. 
Under other assumptions, the population would survive and possibly drift away from a population that remains fairly isolated due to hospitable conditions.
Under long-distance migration, the Sahelian population could even interbreed with more distant populations introducing new alleles in the local genomic picture.

Most theories allow for the survival of part of the population that would not significantly change its behaviour while staying in place. 
The most extreme version of this survival, that is not generally considered to be possible, would involve the population being isolated from all neighbouring populations and experiencing huge swings in population sizes.
This would be reflected by drastic seasonal changes in term of diversity for such a population as rare variants would be in excess as the population rapidly expands during the early wet season.
We do not observe such changes in diversity statistics, which is not unexpected, either among the Sahelian cohorts or among cohorts that are not expected to need to aestivate or migrate to maintain the population.
We can, however, observe that for most of the more coastal populations Tajima's D is less negative, which may indicate that there is less variation in term in population size, which is consistent with longer wet seasons and less need for a coping mechanism for the dry seasons.

It is not obvious at this point what would be the expected effect of the various possible behaviours under consideration on diversity statistics.
For this reason, these do not clearly point us towards a theory as being more likely than the others.
However, if we assume that in non-Sahelian locations, aestivation or migration is not necessary, it is of interest that no clear distinction can be found between the Sahelian cohorts and the non-Sahelian cohorts.
In a similar fashion, _Anopheles coluzzii_ and _Anopheles gambiae s.s._ cohorts yield very similar values for the diversity statistics.
It is possible that both taxa handle the dry season in similar ways.
It is also possible that the impact of aestivation or migration on diversity statistics is too small to be detected. 
The diversity statistics are much lower for the Guinean _An. coluzzii_ population but it might be due to a different population history instead of a different handling of seasonal differences.

We also observe a slightly more negative Tajima's D for the cohort composed of the aerial samples. 
It is possible that this is due to the fact that this cohort is less homogeneous than cohorts that were collected on the ground as it is quite possible that the aerial cohorts came from very different locations than the ones where they were collected.
It is also possible that the sub-population that migrates is actually resulting from a quickly expanding population causing an increased number of rare variants.
